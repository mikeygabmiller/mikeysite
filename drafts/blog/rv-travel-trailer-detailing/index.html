RV and Travel Trailer Detailing

What an RV's outside is made of, why it goes chalky, what to do before it goes into storage for the winter, and how to get a real price for yours.

By Mikey · November 2026

Do you detail RVs and travel trailers?

Yes: RVs, travel trailers and horse trailers, quoted per job from photos. Size, the shape it's in and what the outside is made of change the work too much for one price. One of my reviews is from a customer who had me do his Silverado and his 28 foot travel trailer on the same visit. An RV isn't a big car, though. The outside is usually a different material, it sits outside for months, and it's tall. Here's what's worth knowing before you book anyone for one.

What the outside is made of

  • Fiberglass with gelcoat. Most newer trailers and motorhomes. Gelcoat is a hard, glossy layer over the fiberglass. It's tougher than car clear coat but it's porous, so it soaks up dirt and oxidizes in the sun.
  • Painted. Some higher-end coaches have full automotive-style paint with clear coat. These get treated like a car.
  • Aluminum siding. Older trailers and a lot of horse trailers. Painted aluminum and bare or polished aluminum need different products; the wrong one leaves marks.

Knowing which you have changes what goes on it and how long it takes. That's the main reason I quote from photos instead of a price list.

Why it goes chalky

Rub your hand on the side of an older trailer and it comes away white. That's oxidation: the sun breaks down the top of the gelcoat into a dull, powdery layer. Light oxidation cleans up and gets protected. Heavy oxidation needs polishing to cut back to fresh gelcoat before anything goes on it, which is a lot more time. If you send me photos, send one of your hand after you've rubbed the side. It tells me more than any description.

The black streaks

Those black runs down the sides, under the windows and from the roof edges, are dirt and roof residue that washes down every time it rains, which around here is most of the year. Regular soap doesn't touch them. They need the right cleaner and some patience, and they come back unless the surface is protected so the next rain slides off.

Before it goes into storage

Fall is when most trailers here get parked for the winter, and putting one away dirty is how a small problem becomes a big one:

  • Wash it before it sits. Bird droppings, tree sap and road grime sitting for five months etch into the finish. Sap especially.
  • Get protection on the outside. A wax or sealant on gelcoat slows down oxidation and makes spring cleaning far easier.
  • Clean the inside and dry it out. Food crumbs bring mice, and damp in a closed-up trailer through a Washington winter is how the musty smell starts.
  • Check the seals and the roof. Cracked sealant around the roof, windows and vents is where water gets in. That's a job for an RV tech, not a detailer, but fall is the time to look.

Inside an RV

Inside is closer to a house than a car: cushions, carpet, a kitchen, a bathroom, and a lot of surfaces. Tell me what you want done in there and I'll tell you what I can do in your driveway and what it costs. Pet hair and smells are priced on how bad they are, same as in a car.

What I need from you

  • Photos: each side, the front and back, a close-up of the worst spot, and your hand after rubbing the side.
  • The length and roughly what year it is.
  • What you want done: outside, inside, or both.
  • Where it's parked. I need to be able to walk all the way around it, and reach an outdoor spigot and an outlet. I use yours, which is how I keep prices down. If it's in a storage lot with no water, tell me up front.
