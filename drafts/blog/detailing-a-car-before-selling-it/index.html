Detailing a Car Before You Sell It

When it pays, when it doesn't, and what a buyer actually notices in the first two minutes. Honest version, from someone who'd be the one doing the detail.

By Mikey · November 2026

Is it worth detailing a car before you sell it?

If you're selling it yourself, usually yes. If you're trading it in, usually not. A private buyer decides a lot from your photos and the first look, and a clean car reads as a car that was looked after. A dealer is going to recondition it anyway and prices the trade-in off the car, not the shine. I'm Mikey, of Mikey's Mobile Detailing in Snohomish, WA, and that's the same thing I'd tell you on the phone.

One of my reviews is from a family who hired me to detail their truck before they sold it, then had me back for their other three cars. I'm not going to tell you a detail adds a certain number of dollars to your price. Nobody honest can, because it depends on the car, the market and the buyer. What I can tell you is what a detail changes, and when it's money well spent.

Private sale: why it usually pays

Someone shopping for a used car is scrolling through a lot of listings that look the same. The ones with clean, bright photos get the clicks. Then when they show up, they're looking for reasons to trust you, or reasons to knock the price down.

  • Smell. It's the first thing a buyer notices when they open the door, and the hardest thing to explain away. Smoke, pets, old food or a damp carpet all say "this car wasn't cared for."
  • Stains on the seats and carpet. A buyer looks at the driver's seat and the floor in front of it before anything else.
  • The door jambs and the cup holders. These are where a quick wash shows. A clean jamb says somebody went past the obvious.
  • Hazy headlights and dull paint. They make a car look older than it is, in person and in photos.

None of that changes how the car runs. It changes how much a buyer trusts what you tell them about how it runs, and how hard they push on price.

Trade-in: usually skip it

A dealership has its own reconditioning on every car it takes in, and the appraiser is looking at the mechanical condition, the miles, the tires and the history. A spotless interior can help a little if the car is a mess, but it rarely moves a trade-in number by what a full detail costs. If you're trading in, a good vacuum and taking your stuff out is enough. Save the money.

The exception: if the car has a smell or stains bad enough that the appraiser will mark it down hard, it can be worth getting just that handled. Text me a photo and I'll tell you honestly whether it's worth it.

What a detail can't fix

  • Dents, rust and paint that's peeling. Those are body shop work.
  • Scratches that go through the clear coat. Polishing makes them less noticeable but doesn't erase them. Here's how to tell which kind you have.
  • Torn seats, burns in the upholstery and cracked dashboards.
  • Anything mechanical. Clean doesn't hide a check-engine light, and a buyer who feels misled walks.

Timing

Get it detailed a day or two before you take the listing photos, not weeks before. Then keep it clean: park it, don't eat in it, and if it rains before a showing, a quick rinse is enough. Take the photos in soft light, an overcast day or the shade, and there's plenty of that around here. Bright sun makes every swirl show up.

What to do yourself first

  • Take everything out: the glovebox, the console, under the seats, the trunk. Buyers open all of it.
  • Find both keys, the owner's manual and any service records. A folder of receipts does as much for trust as a clean car does.
  • Fix the little things that are cheap: a burned-out bulb, wiper blades, the tire that's always low.
