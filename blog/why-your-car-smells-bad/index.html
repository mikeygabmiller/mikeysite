Why Your Car Smells Bad (And How to Fix It)

An air freshener covers a smell for a week. Here's what's causing it, what you can fix at home, and when it needs extraction.

By Mikey · first posted April 2026, updated October 2026

Why does my car smell bad, and how do I get rid of it?

Most car smells come from something soaked into the carpet or seats: water in the padding (musty), smoke in every surface, spilled food or milk, or oil from a dog's coat. An air freshener only covers it. The fix is finding the source and drying it out, and anything that's been there for weeks needs hot water extraction and sanitizing. I'm Mikey, of Mikey's Mobile Detailing in Snohomish, WA, and that's how I get them out.

The air freshener on your mirror isn't fixing anything. It covers the smell for a week or so, then it fades and you're back where you started, with a car that smells like stale pine and whatever was there before.

Here are the smells I deal with most, and what gets rid of them.

Musty or mildew

The big one around here, for obvious reasons. Water gets in from wet shoes, a window left cracked in the rain or a spill that never dried, and it soaks into the carpet padding. You can't see it, but that's where the smell grows.

At home: pull the mats and press on the carpet underneath. If it's damp, it has to dry out completely: windows cracked, a fan on the carpet, a day or two in a garage. Once it's dry, sprinkle baking soda, leave it an hour and vacuum it up.

When that's not enough: if the smell has been there for weeks, it's in the padding and maybe the seat foam, and surface cleaning won't reach it. That needs hot water extraction to pull it out of the padding, then sanitizing.

Smoke

The hardest smell there is. Smoke gets into everything: the headliner, the seat belts, the carpet, the vents, even the hard plastic.

At home: wiping the hard surfaces with a vinegar mix and shampooing the seats will cut it down, but in a car someone smoked in for years it won't get rid of it.

What it takes: every surface cleaned, top to bottom, and a heavily smoked car can take two sessions. I'll tell you what's realistic before I start. Buying a used car that smells like smoke on the test drive? Budget for a deep clean. It won't go away on its own.

Food

Old fries under the seat, a milkshake that wasn't cleaned up, leftovers that leaked in the trunk. Food smells usually have one source.

At home: find it. Check under the seats, in the seat pockets and the trunk. Clean the spot with an all-purpose cleaner, let it dry completely, and leave baking soda on the carpet overnight.

When to call me: if it soaked into the carpet or a seat and sat for a while, bacteria are growing in it, and it needs extraction and sanitizing. Milk is the worst. Milk in a carpet for a week in a warm car is one of the worst smells I deal with.

Wet dog

Dog owners know it. The hair is half of it. The other half is the oil from their coat that rubs into the seats and carpet and breaks down over time.

At home: an enzyme pet odor cleaner from a pet store works better than a regular cleaner, because it breaks down what's causing the smell. Spray, wait as long as the label says, blot.

What I do: extraction on the fabric, which pulls the oils out, then sanitizing. If your dog rides along every week, an interior every 3 to 4 months keeps it in check.

The smell you can't name

Sometimes it's just a general funk. In my experience that's body oil in the seats, crumbs in the cracks and a little moisture in the carpet: months of life adding up. It's honestly the easiest one, because a thorough interior gets all of it at once.
